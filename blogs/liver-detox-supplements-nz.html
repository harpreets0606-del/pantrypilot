Key Takeaways
  • Your liver detoxifies itself 24/7 through Phase 1, Phase 2 and Phase 3 enzyme pathways. Supplements support those pathways — they do not "flush" or "reset" a healthy liver.
  • What the evidence supports: milk thistle (silymarin) for liver-cell protection; soluble fibre and adequate hydration for waste elimination; probiotics for the gut-liver axis. Each has limitations — none is a cure-all.
  • What the evidence does not support: detox teas (mostly senna laxatives), foot detox pads, ionic foot baths, and 3- to 7-day juice cleanses.
  • For New Zealanders, the highest-impact "detox" is alcohol moderation. Alcohol drives roughly 25% of alcohol-attributable deaths in NZ from conditions including liver cirrhosis.

What "liver detox" actually means

Liver detoxification is the multi-step biological process by which your liver neutralises and prepares harmful substances for elimination from the body. It runs continuously, in every healthy person, regardless of what supplements or teas you take. The marketing term "detox" — usually a tea, juice cleanse or capsule kit — describes commercial products, not the biology.

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) and the British Dietetic Association have both concluded that "detox" diets and cleanses are largely a marketing concept, with very little clinical evidence behind their specific claims. Klein and Kiat's 2015 critical review in the Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics looked at eight popular commercial detox programs and found "very little clinical evidence" to support them.

The three phases of real liver detoxification

In healthy biology, your liver processes toxins through three sequential phases. Understanding them matters because supplement ingredients target different phases — and this is why "milk thistle vs NAC vs probiotics" is not an apples-to-apples comparison.

Note what this means in practice: a supplement that "supports liver detox" is really doing one of three things — providing antioxidants for Phase 1, supplying conjugation substrates for Phase 2, or aiding elimination in Phase 3. None of them speeds up the liver beyond its biological capacity. None of them "flushes" stored toxins. The healthy liver simply doesn't store toxins waiting to be released, as Johns Hopkins Medicine notes.

Liver health in New Zealand: the picture

Liver disease is more common in Aotearoa than most Kiwis realise, and the burden falls unevenly.

  • About 75% of people with obesity have fatty liver (Hepatitis Foundation of NZ), with higher rates among Māori, Pacific peoples, Indian and Chinese New Zealanders.
  • 28.5% of NZ men with obesity have elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT), a marker of liver-cell stress, in the most recent NZ national survey.
  • 16.6% of New Zealand adults drink at hazardous levels (AUDIT ≥ 8) according to the 2024/25 NZ Health Survey — down from 21.3% in 2019/20.
  • Alcohol is attributed to roughly 900 deaths and 29,282 hospitalisations in New Zealand each year, University of Otago researchers report, with 25% of those deaths from conditions including liver cirrhosis, pancreatitis and epilepsy. Māori experience around twice the alcohol-attributable mortality rate of non-Māori.
  • The total annual cost of alcohol harm in NZ was estimated at NZ$9.1 billion in the 2024 NZIER report.

This matters because the strongest "liver detox" intervention available in New Zealand is not a supplement — it is alcohol moderation paired with weight management. Supplements can play a supportive role; they are not a substitute.

What the science actually says — ingredient by ingredient

Here is how the most common ingredients in NZ-sold liver detox products rank against the published evidence, summarised at-a-glance and then explained one by one.

IngredientEvidence strengthWhat the highest-quality evidence shows
Soluble fibre (psyllium) Strong Supports daily bowel transit and elimination of bile-bound waste in Phase 3. The most rigorously studied "detox-adjacent" ingredient.
Adequate hydration Strong Required for kidney clearance of water-soluble Phase 2 conjugates. Te Whatu Ora recommends 2.8 L/day women, 3.4 L/day men in total fluids.
Multi-strain probiotics Moderate A 2024 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs in alcoholic liver disease found probiotics significantly reduced ALT, AST and γ-GT. Human RCT evidence in healthy adults is more limited.
Milk thistle (silymarin) Moderate The Cochrane Review (Rambaldi 2007, 13 RCTs / 915 patients) found no clear effect on mortality, but later mechanistic and antioxidant studies (Dhande 2024) support liver-cell protective effects with a strong safety profile.
NAC (N-acetylcysteine) / glutathione Moderate NAC is the established hospital treatment for paracetamol overdose because it replenishes glutathione, the Phase 2 conjugation substrate. Oral NAC for general "detox" has less robust evidence but is biochemically plausible.
Globe artichoke (cynarin) Limited Small trials suggest it may stimulate bile flow. Traditionally used; modern clinical evidence is limited but generally well tolerated.
Turmeric / curcumin Limited Anti-inflammatory in mechanism, but high-dose turmeric is now documented as a cause of supplement-induced liver injury. Use modest doses.
Dandelion root Limited Traditionally used as a bitter to support digestion. Minimal modern clinical trial data.
Detox teas (senna-based) Insufficient Senna is a stimulant laxative. Short-term "weight loss" is water and stool, not fat or toxins. Daily use risks dehydration and laxative dependence.
Foot detox pads / ionic foot baths Insufficient No plausible mechanism. The brown discolouration is an electrode-and-water reaction independent of feet being present.

An analysis presented at an American College of Gastroenterology meeting identified 65 unique ingredients across popular liver-cleanse products — and none of them is endorsed by the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) or European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) for treating liver disease. The lesson is not that all ingredients are useless, but that "liver support" claims sit on a spectrum from "biochemically plausible with some clinical evidence" through to "marketing with nothing behind it".

The unsexy truth: The "supplements" with the strongest evidence for supporting your body's detoxification are the cheapest — adequate fibre, adequate water, adequate sleep, and reducing alcohol within the Health New Zealand low-risk guidelines. Capsules and teas can play a supporting role; they are never a substitute.

What the evidence does not support

Some products marketed as "detox" have failed to show meaningful clinical benefit, or have mechanisms that don't stand up to scrutiny.

Detox teas with senna or cascara are stimulant laxatives. They cause short-term water and stool loss, not toxin removal. Daily use is associated with dehydration, electrolyte disturbance and laxative dependence.

Foot detox pads and ionic foot baths. The brown discolouration sold as "extracted toxins" is the chemical reaction between the pad ingredients (or bath electrodes) and water. Multiple independent investigations confirm the colour change happens whether feet are in the water or not.

Juice cleanses. A 2025 study in Nutrients found that even a short juice cleanse increased pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria in the gut microbiome and disrupted oral microbial composition. Short-term weight loss is water and glycogen, regained within days.

How to choose a quality liver detox supplement: a 5-point pharmacy checklist

If you do choose to take a liver-support supplement, here is the framework our pharmacy team uses when answering customer questions across the counter.

What to checkWhat to look for
1. Active ingredient and dose Look for a named, standardised extract — for example, "milk thistle 35,000 mg fresh-equivalent, standardised to 80% silymarin". Vague "proprietary blends" hide the actual dose.
2. Evidence base Cross-check the active ingredient against the NCCIH fact sheet or NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. If you can find no peer-reviewed evidence, treat the claim with caution.
3. Manufacturing standard Prefer brands manufactured to GMP standard. NZ-stocked options include Clinicians, Blackmores, Good Health, Nutra-Life and Thompson's.
4. Honest claims Compliant phrasing is "may support healthy liver function". Walk away from anything claiming to "cure", "flush" or "reset" an organ. Medsafe prohibits therapeutic claims on dietary supplements.
5. Interaction profile Several "detox" herbs interact with prescription medicines — especially anticoagulants, statins, oral contraceptives and immunosuppressants. Always ask a pharmacist before starting one (see the next section).
Red flags to avoid: "Rapid 3-day liver flush", "reverses fatty liver", "eliminates toxins overnight", undisclosed proprietary blends, products promoted only via social-media influencers, or programmes that require fasting for more than 24 hours without medical supervision.

Drug interactions and who should not take liver detox supplements

This is the section most "best liver detox" articles in New Zealand miss entirely. A registered pharmacist's first concern when a customer reaches for a herbal liver product is not efficacy — it is safety in combination with their prescription medicines. Houston Methodist's October 2025 review reminds us that "natural" does not equal "safe": supplements including green-tea extract, high-dose turmeric and black cohosh are now well-documented causes of supplement-induced liver injury.

In 2020, the NZ Science Media Centre reported on a high-profile NZ case where a popular green-tea-extract weight-loss supplement was withdrawn after reports of liver harm.

Herb or activeKnown or theoretical interaction
Milk thistle (silymarin) Inhibits CYP2C9 and other liver enzymes. May affect blood thinners (warfarin), some statins, diabetes medications and certain anti-epileptics. Generally well tolerated; ask your pharmacist if you take any prescription medicine.
Turmeric / curcumin Antiplatelet effect — may increase bleeding risk with warfarin, dabigatran, aspirin or clopidogrel. High-dose extracts have been linked to liver injury in case reports.
Globe artichoke Stimulates bile flow — avoid in known bile-duct obstruction or active gallstones. Generally well tolerated otherwise.
Dandelion root Mild diuretic — caution with prescription diuretics, lithium, and in people with kidney disease.
NAC (N-acetylcysteine) Generally safe at supplement doses. May potentiate the effect of nitrates (e.g. GTN) — discuss with your pharmacist if you have a heart condition.
Green-tea extract (high-dose EGCG) Documented cases of hepatotoxicity. Avoid high-dose extracts (more than ~800 mg EGCG/day). Whole green tea as a drink is safe.
Senna / cascara (in detox teas) Stimulant laxative. Daily use depletes potassium — risk with diuretics, digoxin and some heart medications.

Do not start a liver detox supplement without speaking to your pharmacist or GP if you

  • Are pregnant or breastfeeding
  • Take any prescription medication — especially blood thinners, oral contraceptives, immunosuppressants, statins, diabetes medications or anti-epileptics
  • Have a known liver, kidney or gallbladder condition
  • Are buying for a child under 12
  • Have yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, persistent fatigue or right-sided abdominal pain — these need clinical assessment, not a supplement

Lifestyle factors that matter more than any supplement

If you take only five things away from this guide, take these.

  1. Stay within Health New Zealand low-risk drinking limits: no more than 10 standard drinks per week for women, 15 for men, with at least two alcohol-free days, per the Health NZ guidance. A standard drink is 10 g of pure alcohol — equivalent to a 330 ml can of 4% beer or a 100 ml glass of 12.5% wine.
  2. Hit your fibre target. NZ adults average around 20 g of dietary fibre per day, but the Eating and Activity Guidelines recommend 28 g/day for women and 38 g/day for men for chronic-disease prevention. Wholegrains, legumes, vegetables and fruit are the easiest sources.
  3. Drink water consistently: roughly 2.8 L/day for women and 3.4 L/day for men of total fluids, ideally as plain tap water.
  4. Move every day. Aerobic exercise increases bile flow, improves insulin sensitivity and reduces hepatic fat deposition — confirmed across multiple systematic reviews.
  5. Sleep 7–9 hours. Deep sleep is when the brain's overnight waste-clearance cycle runs at full capacity. See our pharmacy team's guide to magnesium for sleep and relaxation for ways to improve sleep quality.

When to talk to your pharmacist or GP

If you experience persistent fatigue, yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), dark urine, pale stools, right-sided abdominal pain or unexplained nausea, please see your GP rather than reach for a supplement. These symptoms warrant clinical assessment and possibly blood tests for liver function.

Frequently asked questions

Do detox supplements actually work?

Some do, in specific ways. Soluble fibre, multi-strain probiotics and milk thistle (silymarin) have meaningful clinical evidence supporting bowel transit, gut-liver axis function and liver-cell protection respectively. Detox teas, foot pads and 3- to 7-day cleanse kits do not have credible evidence behind their toxin-removal claims.

Does my liver actually need cleansing?

A healthy liver does not need cleansing — it cleanses itself continuously through Phase 1, Phase 2 and Phase 3 enzyme pathways. Supplements can support those pathways, particularly when alcohol intake, diet or medications place extra demand on the liver. They are not a substitute for the body's existing detoxification system.

What does milk thistle do, and is there evidence it works?

Milk thistle's active compound, silymarin, has antioxidant properties and is studied for protecting liver cells from oxidative damage. The NCCIH describes the evidence as "promising" for supportive use, while the Cochrane Review found no clear effect on liver-disease mortality. Silymarin has a strong safety profile.

How long do you need to take milk thistle to see results?

Clinical studies measuring biochemical markers (ALT, AST) typically run for 8–12 weeks. If you take milk thistle for general liver support, give it at least 8 weeks at the dose stated on the label, alongside lifestyle changes. If you take it for a clinical reason, talk with your pharmacist or GP about appropriate duration.

Are there side effects or medication interactions I should know about?

Milk thistle is generally well tolerated. It can affect medicines metabolised by the CYP2C9 enzyme — including warfarin, some statins and certain diabetes medications. High-dose turmeric and high-dose green-tea extract have been linked to liver injury in case reports. Always speak with your pharmacist before starting a supplement if you take any prescription medication.

Are detox teas safe — and do they help with weight loss?

Most detox teas sold in New Zealand contain senna or cascara, which are stimulant laxatives. Short-term weight loss is from water and stool, not body fat or toxins. Daily use can cause dehydration, electrolyte imbalance and laxative dependence. They are safe for occasional short-term use as laxatives, but not as a wellness habit.

What are the signs that my liver might be struggling?

Persistent fatigue, yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, pale stools, right-sided abdominal pain, easy bruising, unexplained itching and abdominal swelling are all reasons to see your GP. These signs need clinical assessment with blood tests — they should not be self-managed with a supplement.

What foods and drinks actually support liver health?

The Hepatitis Foundation of New Zealand highlights leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, kale, cabbage), berries, fatty fish, nuts and olive oil. These provide antioxidants and phase-2 enzyme cofactors. Coffee in moderation is also associated with lower liver-enzyme levels in observational studies.

How often should I do a liver detox?

The honest pharmacist answer is that ongoing daily lifestyle support — fibre, hydration, lower alcohol, exercise and sleep — is more beneficial than any periodic "detox programme". If you choose to take a liver-support supplement, most NZ products are designed for daily use over one to three months. There is no medical reason to do a 7-day cleanse.

Can supplements help with a hangover or protect my liver when I drink?

Evidence for hangover-prevention supplements is limited. The most effective recovery is water, sleep, food and time. No supplement reverses alcohol's effects on the liver. If you regularly exceed Health New Zealand's low-risk drinking guidelines (10 standard drinks per week for women, 15 for men), the single most beneficial action is to reduce intake.

Can a supplement reverse fatty liver disease?

No supplement is approved to treat or reverse fatty liver disease in New Zealand. The strongest evidence for reversing non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is sustained weight loss (typically 7–10% of body weight), increased physical activity, and reducing alcohol and refined carbohydrates. If you have been diagnosed with fatty liver, please discuss management with your GP rather than self-medicate.

What ingredients should I look for in a liver supplement?

Prioritise standardised milk thistle (silymarin) at a stated dose, optionally combined with NAC (a glutathione precursor) and/or globe artichoke for bile flow. Avoid undisclosed proprietary blends, very high-dose turmeric extracts, and stimulant-laxative-based "detox teas" marketed for daily long-term use.
