Liver Detox Supplements NZ: A Pharmacist's Evidence-Based Guide (2026)

Milk thistle, NAC, globe artichoke and the rest. What the science actually supports for liver health in New Zealand.

Key Takeaways
  • Your liver detoxifies itself 24/7 through three enzyme pathways called Phase 1, Phase 2 and Phase 3. Supplements support those pathways. They do not "flush" or "reset" a healthy liver.
  • What the evidence supports: milk thistle (silymarin) for liver-cell protection, soluble fibre and water for waste elimination, and probiotics for gut and liver health. Each has limits. None is a cure-all.
  • What the evidence does not support: detox teas (mostly senna laxatives), foot detox pads, ionic foot baths, and 3 to 7 day juice cleanses.
  • For New Zealanders, the highest-impact "detox" is drinking less alcohol. Alcohol drives about 25% of alcohol-related deaths in NZ from conditions like liver cirrhosis.

The combined Australia and NZ dietary supplements market is forecast to grow from USD 3.60 billion in 2024 to USD 6.55 billion by 2030. So what works, what does not, and what should Kiwi consumers actually look for?

What "liver detox" actually means

Liver detoxification is the multi-step process your liver uses to neutralise harmful substances and prepare them to leave the body. It runs all the time, in every healthy person, no matter what supplements or teas you take. The word "detox" in marketing usually means a tea, juice cleanse or capsule kit. That is a product, not a biological process.

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) and the British Dietetic Association both find that "detox" diets and cleanses are largely a marketing concept. There is very little clinical evidence behind their specific claims. Klein and Kiat's 2015 review in the Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics looked at eight popular commercial detox programmes. They found "very little clinical evidence" to support them.

The three phases of real liver detoxification

In healthy biology, your liver processes toxins through three steps. Each phase uses different enzymes and substrates. Different supplement ingredients support different phases. This is why "milk thistle vs NAC vs probiotics" is not an apples-to-apples comparison.

Here is what this means in plain terms. A supplement that "supports liver detox" is really doing one of three things. It provides antioxidants for Phase 1, supplies building blocks for Phase 2, or aids elimination in Phase 3. None of them speeds up the liver beyond its normal capacity. None of them "flushes" stored toxins. The healthy liver does not store toxins waiting to be released, as Johns Hopkins Medicine notes.

Liver health in New Zealand: the picture

Liver disease is more common in Aotearoa than most Kiwis realise. The burden also falls unevenly.

  • About 75% of people with obesity have fatty liver, according to the Hepatitis Foundation of NZ. Rates are higher among Māori, Pacific peoples, Indian and Chinese New Zealanders.
  • 28.5% of NZ men with obesity have raised alanine aminotransferase (ALT), which is a marker of liver-cell stress. This is from the most recent NZ national survey.
  • 16.6% of New Zealand adults drink at hazardous levels (AUDIT ≥ 8) in the 2024/25 NZ Health Survey. That is down from 21.3% in 2019/20.
  • Alcohol is linked to around 900 deaths and 29,282 hospitalisations in New Zealand each year, University of Otago researchers report. About 25% of those deaths are from conditions like liver cirrhosis, pancreatitis and epilepsy. Māori experience around twice the alcohol-related death rate of non-Māori.
  • The total annual cost of alcohol harm in NZ was put at NZ$9.1 billion in the 2024 NZIER report.

These statistics describe a public-health context. Dietary supplements are not a treatment for liver disease, alcohol harm or cirrhosis. The most useful steps for liver health are drinking less alcohol, eating well and staying active.

If you are worried about your alcohol use, please contact your GP or the Alcohol Drug Helpline on 0800 787 797. Free, confidential, available 7 days.

What the science actually says, ingredient by ingredient

Here is how the most common ingredients in NZ-sold liver detox products rank against the published evidence. The table summarises each one. The text below explains the highlights.

IngredientEvidence strengthWhat the highest-quality evidence shows
Soluble fibre (psyllium) Strong Supports daily bowel transit and elimination of bile-bound waste in Phase 3. The most heavily studied "detox-adjacent" ingredient.
Adequate hydration Strong Needed for the kidneys to clear water-soluble waste from Phase 2. Te Whatu Ora recommends about 2.1 L per day for women and 2.6 L for men in total fluids.
Multi-strain probiotics Moderate Probiotic supplementation has been studied for its role in the gut and liver axis, with most published trial evidence in patient populations rather than healthy adults. A 2024 systematic review summarises the current state of the evidence.
Milk thistle (silymarin) Moderate The Cochrane Review (Rambaldi 2007, 13 RCTs / 915 patients) found no clear effect on death rates. Later mechanism studies (Dhande 2024) support liver-cell protection with a strong safety profile.
NAC (N-acetylcysteine) / glutathione Moderate NAC is a precursor to glutathione, the substrate used in Phase 2 conjugation. Evidence for oral NAC as a general dietary supplement is limited, but the mechanism is plausible.
Globe artichoke (cynarin) Limited Small trials suggest it may stimulate bile flow. Traditionally used and generally well tolerated. Modern clinical evidence is limited.
Turmeric / curcumin Limited Anti-inflammatory in mechanism. However, high-dose turmeric is now documented as a cause of supplement-related liver injury. Stick to modest doses.
Dandelion root Limited Traditionally used as a bitter to support digestion. Minimal modern trial data.
Detox teas (senna-based) Insufficient Senna is a stimulant laxative. Short-term "weight loss" is water and stool, not fat or toxins. Daily use can lead to dehydration and laxative dependence.
Foot detox pads / ionic foot baths Insufficient No plausible mechanism. The brown colour is from a chemical reaction between the pad or electrodes and water. It happens with or without feet in the water.

An analysis presented at an American College of Gastroenterology meeting identified 65 unique ingredients across popular liver-cleanse products. None of them is endorsed by the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) or the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) for treating liver disease. The lesson is not that every ingredient is useless. It is that "liver support" claims sit on a long spectrum. Some have decent evidence. Others are pure marketing.

The unsexy truth. The "supplements" with the strongest evidence for supporting your body's detox work are also the cheapest. Enough fibre. Enough water. Enough sleep. Less alcohol, within the Health New Zealand low-risk guidelines. Capsules and teas can play a supporting role. They are never a substitute.

What the evidence does not support

Some products marketed as "detox" have failed to show clear clinical benefit. Others use mechanisms that simply do not stand up to scrutiny.

Detox teas with senna or cascara are stimulant laxatives. They cause short-term water and stool loss. They do not remove toxins. Daily use is linked to dehydration, electrolyte problems and laxative dependence.

Foot detox pads and ionic foot baths. The brown discolouration sold as "extracted toxins" is just the chemical reaction between the pad ingredients (or bath electrodes) and water. Multiple independent tests confirm the colour change happens whether or not feet are in the water.

Juice cleanses. A 2025 study in Nutrients found that even a short juice cleanse raised pro-inflammatory bacteria in the gut and disrupted oral microbe balance. Any short-term weight loss is water and glycogen, regained within days.

How to choose a quality liver detox supplement: a 5-point pharmacy checklist

If you decide to take a liver-support supplement, here is the framework our pharmacy team uses when customers ask across the counter.

What to checkWhat to look for
1. Active ingredient and dose Look for a named, standardised extract. For example, "milk thistle 35,000 mg fresh-equivalent, standardised to 80% silymarin". Vague "proprietary blends" hide the actual dose.
2. Evidence base Check the active ingredient against the NCCIH fact sheet or the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. If you cannot find any peer-reviewed evidence, treat the claim with caution.
3. Manufacturing standard Prefer brands made to GMP standard. NZ-stocked options include Clinicians, Blackmores, Good Health, Nutra-Life and Thompson's.
4. Honest claims Compliant phrasing is "may support healthy liver function". Walk away from anything claiming to "cure", "flush" or "reset" an organ. Medsafe prohibits therapeutic claims on dietary supplements.
5. Interaction profile Several "detox" herbs interact with prescription medicines. The main ones to watch are blood thinners, statins, the oral contraceptive pill and medicines that suppress the immune system. Always ask a pharmacist before starting one. See the next section.
Red flags to avoid: "Rapid 3-day liver flush", "reverses fatty liver", "eliminates toxins overnight", undisclosed proprietary blends, products only sold through social-media influencers, or programmes that require fasting for more than 24 hours without medical supervision.

Drug interactions and who should not take liver detox supplements

This is the section most "best liver detox" articles in New Zealand miss. A registered pharmacist's first concern when you reach for a herbal liver product is not how well it works. It is whether it is safe with your prescription medicines. Houston Methodist's October 2025 review is a useful reminder that "natural" does not equal "safe". Supplements like green-tea extract, high-dose turmeric and black cohosh are now well-documented causes of supplement-related liver injury.

In 2020, the NZ Science Media Centre reported on a high-profile NZ case where a popular green-tea-extract weight-loss supplement was withdrawn after reports of liver harm.

Please note. The table below lists known or theoretical interactions. It is general information only, not a complete list, and not medical advice. Always bring the supplement packaging to your pharmacist for a full check against your individual medicines.
Herb or activeKnown or theoretical interaction
Milk thistle (silymarin) Affects CYP2C9 and some other liver enzymes. May change the effect of blood thinners (warfarin), some statins, certain diabetes medicines and some anti-epileptic medicines. Generally well tolerated. Ask your pharmacist if you take any prescription medicine.
Turmeric / curcumin Acts on platelets, so it may raise bleeding risk with warfarin, dabigatran, aspirin or clopidogrel. High-dose extracts have been linked to liver injury in case reports.
Globe artichoke Stimulates bile flow. Avoid if you have a known bile-duct obstruction or active gallstones. Generally well tolerated otherwise.
Dandelion root Mild diuretic. Use with care alongside prescription diuretics, lithium, or if you have kidney disease.
NAC (N-acetylcysteine) Generally safe at supplement doses. May add to the effect of nitrates such as GTN. Discuss with your pharmacist if you have a heart condition.
Green-tea extract (high-dose EGCG) Documented cases of liver harm. Avoid high-dose extracts above roughly 800 mg EGCG per day. Drinking whole green tea is safe.
Senna / cascara (in detox teas) Stimulant laxative. Daily use can lower potassium. That is a risk with diuretics, digoxin and some heart medicines.

Do not start a liver detox supplement without speaking to your pharmacist or GP if you:

  • Are pregnant or breastfeeding
  • Take any prescription medicine, especially blood thinners, the oral contraceptive pill, medicines that suppress the immune system, statins, diabetes medicines or anti-epileptics
  • Have a known liver, kidney or gallbladder condition
  • Are buying for a child under 12
  • Have yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, lasting tiredness or right-sided tummy pain. These signs need clinical assessment, not a supplement.

Lifestyle factors that matter more than any supplement

If you take only five things away from this guide, take these.

  1. Stay within Health New Zealand low-risk drinking limits. That is no more than 10 standard drinks per week for women and 15 for men. Aim for at least two alcohol-free days each week, per the Health NZ guidance. A standard drink is 10 g of pure alcohol. That is about a 330 ml can of 4% beer or a 100 ml glass of 12.5% wine.
  2. Hit your fibre target. NZ adults average around 20 g of dietary fibre per day. The Eating and Activity Guidelines recommend 28 g per day for women and 38 g per day for men for chronic-disease prevention. Wholegrains, legumes, vegetables and fruit are the easiest sources.
  3. Drink water consistently. Aim for about 2.1 L per day for women and 2.6 L for men in total fluids. Plain tap water is ideal.
  4. Move every day. Regular exercise increases bile flow, improves how your body uses insulin and reduces fat in the liver. This is confirmed across many large reviews.
  5. Sleep 7 to 9 hours. Deep sleep is when the brain's overnight waste-clearance cycle runs at full speed. See our pharmacy team's guide to magnesium for sleep and relaxation for ways to improve your sleep quality.

When to talk to your pharmacist or GP

If you have lasting tiredness, yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), dark urine, pale stools, right-sided tummy pain or unexplained nausea, please see your GP rather than reach for a supplement. These signs need clinical assessment and possibly blood tests for liver function.

Frequently asked questions

Do detox supplements actually work?

Some do, in specific ways. Soluble fibre, multi-strain probiotics and milk thistle (silymarin) have meaningful clinical evidence behind them for bowel transit, gut and liver health, and liver-cell protection. Detox teas, foot pads and 3 to 7 day cleanse kits do not have credible evidence behind their toxin-removal claims.

Does my liver actually need cleansing?

A healthy liver does not need cleansing. It cleanses itself all the time through Phase 1, Phase 2 and Phase 3 enzyme pathways. Supplements can support those pathways, especially if alcohol, diet or medicines place extra demand on the liver. They are not a substitute for the body's own detox system.

What does milk thistle do, and is there evidence it works?

Milk thistle's active compound, silymarin, has antioxidant properties. It is studied for protecting liver cells from oxidative damage. The NCCIH describes the evidence as "promising" for supportive use. The Cochrane Review found no clear effect on liver-disease mortality. Silymarin has a strong safety profile.

How long do you need to take milk thistle to see results?

Clinical studies that measure liver blood markers (ALT, AST) usually run for 8 to 12 weeks. If you take milk thistle for general liver support, give it at least 8 weeks at the dose on the label, alongside lifestyle changes. If you take it for a specific clinical reason, talk with your pharmacist or GP about an appropriate length.

Are there side effects or medicine interactions I should know about?

Milk thistle is generally well tolerated. It can affect medicines processed by the CYP2C9 enzyme, including warfarin, some statins and certain diabetes medicines. High-dose turmeric and high-dose green-tea extract have been linked to liver injury in case reports. Always speak with your pharmacist before starting a supplement if you take any prescription medicine.

Are detox teas safe, and do they help with weight loss?

Most detox teas sold in New Zealand contain senna or cascara, which are stimulant laxatives. Short-term weight loss is from water and stool, not body fat or toxins. Daily use can cause dehydration, electrolyte imbalance and laxative dependence. They are safe for occasional short-term use as a laxative, but not as a wellness habit.

What are the signs that my liver might be struggling?

Lasting tiredness, yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, pale stools, right-sided tummy pain, easy bruising, unexplained itching and abdominal swelling are all reasons to see your GP. These signs need clinical assessment with blood tests. They should not be self-managed with a supplement.

What foods and drinks actually support liver health?

The Hepatitis Foundation of New Zealand highlights leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, kale, cabbage), berries, fatty fish, nuts and olive oil. These provide antioxidants and Phase 2 building blocks. Coffee in moderation is also linked to lower liver enzyme levels in observational studies.

How often should I do a liver detox?

The honest pharmacist answer is that ongoing daily habits beat any periodic "detox programme". Fibre, water, less alcohol, exercise and sleep do more than any kit. If you choose to take a liver-support supplement, most NZ products are designed for daily use over one to three months. There is no medical reason to do a 7-day cleanse.

Can supplements help with a hangover or protect my liver when I drink?

Evidence for hangover-prevention supplements is limited. The most effective recovery is water, sleep, food and time. No supplement reverses alcohol's effects on the liver. If you regularly exceed Health New Zealand's low-risk drinking guidelines (10 standard drinks per week for women, 15 for men), the single most useful action is to cut back.

Can a supplement reverse fatty liver disease?

No supplement is approved to treat or reverse fatty liver disease in New Zealand. The strongest evidence for reversing non-alcoholic fatty liver is steady weight loss (typically 7 to 10% of body weight), more physical activity, and cutting back on alcohol and refined carbohydrates. If you have been diagnosed with fatty liver, please talk to your GP rather than self-treat.

What ingredients should I look for in a liver supplement?

Look for products with a clearly stated active ingredient and dose. For example, standardised silymarin from milk thistle at a named extract strength. Avoid hidden proprietary blends, very high-dose turmeric extracts, and stimulant-laxative-based "detox teas" marketed for daily long-term use.
